## What you'll be able to do — and why it matters

When the [barangay](/flood-ready/) or your LGU finally says it's safe to go back, that clearance covers the streets and the general area — it does not mean your specific house is safe to walk into, plug in, or let your kids run around in. Those are two different kinds of "safe," and mixing them up is how people get hurt after the water has already gone down.

This guide walks you through the inspection you do before you actually move back in: checking the structure from outside, checking the electrical panel before anyone touches a switch, checking for gas leaks, checking whether your water is drinkable, and knowing what the mud left behind can do to you. None of this is about cleanup — that's a separate job for another day. This is about the twenty minutes before you cross the threshold, done in the right order, so the house you're returning to doesn't have a second disaster waiting in it.

I think about this the way I used to think about entering a scene after a callout — the emergency everyone can see is often not the one that gets someone hurt. It's the thing nobody checked first.

## What you need

You don't need much for this, and that's the point — you need a flashlight or headlamp with fresh batteries (not the flooded one that was sitting in your go bag under a meter of water), rubber boots or closed shoes, rubber gloves, a stick or pole for probing, and your phone charged enough to make calls. If you have a battery gas leak detector or a simple continuity tester, bring it, but they are not required — your nose and your eyes, used carefully, do most of the work here.

If you haven't already got a proper flood go-bag stocked with these basics, it's worth building one before the next system forms — <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> will give you the lead time, but only if you're already packed.

## The method

**1. Check with your barangay DRRMO or LGU first.**
Before you even walk up your street, confirm the area-wide clearance covers your specific block, especially if you're near a creek, a riverbank, or in a low-lying pocket that floods worse than the rest of the barangay. If the flood was severe — chest-deep or higher, or if there was visible current — ask if structural assessors have already gone through your row of houses. Some LGUs tag homes with a colored marker after a rapid assessment. Look for one before you assume you're clear.

**2. Do the exterior check before you go inside.**
Walk around the outside of the house first. Look at the foundation line — is there a gap that wasn't there before, does the house look tilted, has soil been washed out from under one corner? Look at the walls for new cracks, especially diagonal ones running from window or door corners, which usually mean the ground under that section moved. Look at posts, stairs, and any attached structures like a garage or laundry area — floodwater with current can undermine footings in a way that only shows up as a lean days later. If anything looks off, don't go in. Call your LGU engineering office or a licensed structural inspector before you or your family cross the doorway. This is also where earthquake-country habits pay off — the same eye you'd use to check a house after a strong shake works here, because the failure mode (a foundation that's lost its footing) looks similar.

**3. Do not touch the electrical panel or any switch until you've called your utility.**
If the breaker box, meter, or any wiring in the house was underwater, do not turn the power back on — not to "just test one light," not even briefly. Water leaves behind corrosion and moisture inside connections that can arc or short well after everything looks dry. Call Meralco (or your local electric cooperative) and report that the panel was submerged. They will tell you whether they need to inspect first, and in many barangays they'll cut and restore service house by house as they clear each one. If your panel was above the flood line but the rest of the house wasn't, still have a licensed electrician check it before anyone flips a breaker. This is not a job to do yourself, and it is not a job to guess about.

**4. Check for gas before you check for anything else that involves a flame or spark.**
Before lighting a match, using a lighter, or even letting a gas heater or stove into service, smell for LPG near your tank and stove connections — it has that distinct rotten-egg smell added specifically so people notice it. If you smell gas, don't turn on lights, don't use your phone inside, don't strike a lighter — leave the area, open no valves, and call the <a href="https://bfp.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bureau of Fire Protection</a> or your LPG supplier from outside the house. Check that your tank valve wasn't knocked open or damaged during the flood, and that the hose hasn't degraded or come loose from being submerged.

**5. Treat all your household water as unsafe until told otherwise.**
Floodwater can get into pipes, wells, and cisterns, and a boil-water advisory from your local water utility or the <a href="https://doh.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Department of Health</a> should be treated as the rule, not a suggestion, until they say otherwise. Boil water for drinking, food prep, and brushing teeth. If you're on a deep well or your own tank, assume it needs testing before use, and don't just trust that it "looks clear" — floodwater often looks clear again long before it's actually safe.

**6. Respect the mud — it's not just dirt.**
Residual mud and debris left behind by floodwater carries real risk, particularly leptospirosis from contact with water or mud contaminated by animal urine. Wear rubber boots and gloves during the inspection itself, cover any cuts or scrapes before you go in, and wash thoroughly afterward. This guide isn't the place for the full leptospirosis picture — we've covered prevention and what to watch for in our leptospirosis articles, and that's worth reading before you start the actual cleanup phase.

## Variations & conditions

A house that took on ankle-deep water for a few hours is a different inspection than one that sat in chest-deep, fast-moving floodwater for a day. The longer and deeper the exposure, and the more current involved, the more you should lean toward professional inspection before re-entry — especially for the structural check. Ground-floor units in a compound or a low-rise apartment building carry an added wrinkle: even if your own unit looks fine, a shared foundation or stairwell that took damage next door can affect you too, so a building-wide clearance matters more than a personal one.

Nighttime return is its own hazard multiplier. If you're arriving after dark, don't try to do a thorough inspection by phone flashlight — wait for daylight if you can, because cracks, tilts, and gas smells are all easier to miss when you're tired and the light is bad. If you must go in at night for something urgent, treat it as a quick retrieval only, not a full check.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is skipping straight past the barangay or LGU check because the water is visibly gone from the street — a receded flood outside doesn't mean the ground under your house has stopped moving or that assessors have reached your row yet. The second is flipping the breaker "just to see" — a submerged panel that seems fine on visual inspection can still be carrying moisture in places you can't see, and that's exactly the kind of thing a licensed electrician is trained to catch. The third is assuming a returned water supply is automatically safe to drink; utilities restore flow well before they confirm quality, and those are announced separately. And the last is treating the mud as a chore rather than a hazard — gloves and boots during the inspection, not just during the cleanup.

## How to practise safely

You can't simulate a flood, but you can build the habit of the checklist itself, and that's most of the value here. Walk your own house now, in dry weather, and physically locate your breaker panel, your gas valve, and your water shutoff — knowing exactly where they are and what they look like when everything is fine makes it much easier to spot when something's wrong later. Talk to your barangay about what their post-flood clearance process actually looks like, so you're not guessing during an actual event. And keep the contact numbers for your electric utility, your LPG supplier, and your barangay DRRMO saved in your phone and written on paper in your go bag — during an actual return-home, you want to be making calls, not searching for numbers.

No inspection checklist replaces professional judgment when the damage is serious. If anything about the structure, the wiring, or the smell of gas doesn't sit right, the safest move is the same one that applies before a flood: wait, and let someone qualified go in first.
