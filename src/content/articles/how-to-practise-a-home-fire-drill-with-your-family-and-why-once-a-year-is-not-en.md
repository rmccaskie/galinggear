## What you'll be able to do — and why it matters

By the end of this, you will have run an actual, timed fire drill with everyone in your household — not just a floor plan taped to the fridge. That is the part most families skip. We have the smoke alarm, we have the extinguisher, we even sketched the exits once on a piece of paper. Then we filed it away and never touched it again.

The paper plan tells you what should happen. Only a drill tells you what actually happens. A drill is the only way to find out that the grillwork on the back window has never been opened, that your five-year-old freezes instead of moving toward the door, or that lola needs almost a full minute just to get out of bed and into her slippers. None of that shows up on paper. It shows up when the alarm actually goes off and you are standing there watching real people move through a real space.

This matters because a working smoke alarm buys you minutes, not hours. Smoke and heat can fill a small room fast — how fast depends on the room's size, what's burning, and how much ventilation there is, so no number here is a guarantee for your house specifically. What matters is the habit, not the stopwatch reading. The families who get out are almost always the ones who didn't have to think — they just moved, because their bodies already knew the route. That is what a drill builds. Not a document. A habit.

March is <a href="https://bfp.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bureau of Fire Protection</a> Fire Prevention Month, and it is as good a hook as any to put your first drill on the calendar. But one drill a year, done once and never repeated, is really just a nice memory. Kids grow, furniture moves, a new tenant changes the stairwell, and skills fade. This is a guide for making the drill a routine, not an event.

## What you need

Nothing here requires new gear. This is about using what you already have — your smoke alarm, your exits, your extinguisher — the way you'll actually need to use it. If you haven't set those up yet, sort that out first; our home fire safety plan article covers choosing and installing a smoke alarm and a fire extinguisher, and where to place them.

For the drill itself, you need:

- **A pen and one sheet of paper per floor**, to sketch a simple floor plan. It does not need to be to scale. It needs to show doors, windows, and two ways out of every room where someone sleeps.
- **A working smoke alarm with a fresh battery**, tested on its test button before you start. If you haven't pressed that button in a few months, do it now — it takes ten seconds and it's the whole reason any of this works.
- **A working flashlight or headlamp** kept somewhere fixed and known — not buried in a drawer. Smoke rises and lights get lost in it; battery power near the floor is what actually helps you see the way out.
- **A phone with a stopwatch**, which every phone already has.
- **A meeting point outside** that everyone, including children, can name and walk to on their own — more on choosing this below.
- **Everyone in the house**, including the household helper, visiting relatives who stay over often, and anyone who is old enough to walk but too young to fully understand what a real fire means.

## The method

### Step 1: Draw a two-exit plan for every room

Walk into each bedroom and ask: if smoke were filling the hallway right now, how would the people in this room get out two different ways? Usually that is the door, plus a window. Sketch it. Mark exactly which window opens, whether the grillwork has a quick-release or needs a key, and where that key is kept. If a window doesn't open, or the grillwork is welded shut with no release, that is not a footnote — that is the single most important thing your drill will have found. Fix it before you do anything else.

Do this room by room, floor by floor. In a two-story townhouse, mark the stairs as the primary route. For a window as the backup route on an upper floor, be honest about whether it is actually usable. A rope ladder or a jump to a carport roof looks fine in a sketch, but it is a real fall-and-injury risk, especially in the dark, in smoke, or for a child or an elderly person. If you plan to rely on one, buy a proper fire-rated escape ladder rated for the drop height, anchor it exactly where you'll use it, and practice climbing down it in daylight, calmly, with no emergency — not the first time you try it during an actual fire. If no one in the house could safely use that window in a real fire, then it isn't really a backup exit. Plan instead to stay in the room with the door closed (see below) and signal from that window for help.

**If you cannot get out.** Sometimes the door is hot, the hallway is already smoky, or the only other way down is one nobody in the room could safely use. Everyone in the household should know what to do here: close the door, put a wet towel or cloth along the gap at the bottom to slow the smoke, go to a window, and call out or wave something bright to signal for help while calling the <a href="https://bfp.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bureau of Fire Protection</a> or 911. Staying put behind a closed door and signaling from a window is often safer than an untested climb down the outside of the house. Put this on the sketch too, as its own labeled step, not just an afterthought.

### Step 2: Pick a meeting point everyone can reach alone

The meeting point should be close enough to walk to quickly, far enough from the house to be safe from heat and falling debris, and specific enough that no one has to guess. "Outside" is not a meeting point. "By the gate of Tita Baby's house next door" is. "The sari-sari store on the corner, standing by the lamppost" is.

Choose it together, out loud, with the kids. Ask your child to point to it and say its name back to you. If a lola or lolo cannot walk that far quickly, choose a nearer point — even just past your own gate — and accept that as the real plan, not the ideal one.

### Step 3: Assign one job to each person

Someone leads the youngest child by the hand. Someone helps lola or lolo, and that person's only job during the drill is that — not grabbing valuables, not looking for the cat. If you have a household helper who lives in, they are part of this, not an afterthought to be told about later. Someone is also responsible for calling the <a href="https://bfp.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bureau of Fire Protection</a> or 911 once everyone is safely out — decide now who that is, so in a real fire no one assumes somebody else already did it. Everyone should also know: once you are out, you stay out. No one goes back in for anything, not for pets, not for valuables, not for a phone left charging on the table.

### Step 4: Run the drill and time it

Press the smoke alarm's test button — never create actual smoke or flame to trigger it — and start the stopwatch at the same moment. If pressing the test button will alarm the whole building or your neighbors, call out "Fire drill — smoke alarm!" loudly enough for the whole house to hear instead, and start the watch then. Everyone moves to their assigned exit, out the door, and to the meeting point. Stop the watch when the last person arrives.

There's no official pass or fail time here, and no validated benchmark for a Philippine household to hit — homes differ too much in size, floor count, and who lives there for one number to mean much. What matters is your own number, tracked over time. The first drill is almost always slower than you'd like. That's the point of doing it: now you know where the time is actually going, instead of guessing. Write it down, and aim to bring it down on the next drill.

### Step 5: Debrief for five minutes, no blame

Sit down right after and talk through what happened. Where did people bunch up? Did anyone hesitate at a door? Did the flashlight actually turn on? Did everyone remember who is responsible for calling the <a href="https://bfp.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bureau of Fire Protection</a> or 911 in a real fire? Write down anything that needs fixing — a sticky window, a blocked hallway, a lock that's hard to work in the dark — and fix it before the next drill, not after the next fire.

## Variations & conditions

**Do it at night, at least once.** A drill in daylight, with everyone alert and dressed, tells you almost nothing about a fire that starts while the household is asleep. Try one with the lights off and only the flashlight in use — not a full unannounced surprise the first time, but tell the family "tonight, after lights out, we're doing our drill in the dark." Getting from bed to the floor to the door without full vision is a completely different skill than walking it in daylight, and it is worth practicing on purpose, calmly, rather than discovering it for the first time during an actual emergency.

**Practice with the "low and slow" crawl.** Smoke rises, and the air near the floor stays clearer longer. Have kids and adults practice getting low — on hands and knees, or a low duck-walk if a full crawl is hard for someone — and moving toward the door, so it isn't a strange new thing if they ever need it for real.

**Adjust for elderly or mobility-limited household members.** If lola uses a cane or moves slowly, the plan is not "she moves faster." The plan is a nearer meeting point, an assigned helper, and possibly a ground-floor bedroom if you can arrange it. A drill that is honest about limits is far more useful than one that pretends everyone moves the same.

**Account for locked gates and grillwork.** Many Philippine homes have security grilles on windows and gates that lock at night. Walk the actual overnight state of your house — which gates are padlocked, where that key sits — because a fire drill that assumes an unlocked gate you actually keep locked every night isn't testing anything real.

**Practice in bad weather too.** If a fire happens during heavy rain or while <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> has a storm signal up, your meeting point might be exposed, or the street might already be flooded. Have a backup meeting point under cover, and know it in advance.

## Common mistakes — and how to avoid them

**Treating the drawing as the drill.** A floor plan on paper is planning. Walking it, with your family, stopwatch running, is the drill. They are not the same thing, and only one of them reveals whether the window actually opens.

**Only ever practicing by day, calm, and expected.** This tests the easiest version of the emergency. Practice the harder version — at night, with the lights off — at least once a year, because that is closer to when it's likely to matter.

**Skipping the youngest and the oldest in the household.** These are exactly the two groups most likely to freeze, panic, or move too slowly in a real fire, which makes them the two who most need to have walked the route before, calmly, as a game, so their bodies already know what to do.

**No named meeting point.** "We'll figure it out outside" wastes minutes you don't have while people search for each other on the street. Name it. Practice walking to it.

**Trusting an untested "backup" window.** A second-floor window with no ladder, no plan, and no practice is not a real exit — it's a hope. If you haven't tested how someone would actually get down from it, treat the room as a "stay and signal" room instead, with the door-closing and towel-at-the-gap steps as the real plan.

**Doing it once and considering the job done.** A drill from a year ago, before the new sofa blocked half the hallway, before your child grew from toddler to preschooler, before lola's knee got worse — that old drill is telling you about a house that no longer exists. Redo it whenever the household or the layout changes, and at minimum, once a year.

**Making it scary.** Fire is a serious thing, but a drill does not need to feel like one. Framing it as a race, a family challenge, or a game — "let's see if we can beat our last time" — gets real cooperation from children without giving them nightmares. Save the serious tone for the debrief, with the adults.

## How to practise safely

Start small and build up. The first time, walk the routes slowly in daylight, no stopwatch, just to get everyone oriented and comfortable with where the exits actually are. Only add the stopwatch, the nighttime version, and the "lights off, flashlight only" version once everyone is used to the basic walk.

Never test with an actual fire, smoke, or flame — press the smoke alarm's test button only, or use the shouted "Fire drill!" version if a real alarm test would disturb the building or neighbors. If you plan to rely on a fire escape ladder as a backup exit, test it in daylight, with no emergency pressure, and only in the way it's rated to be used — not as a surprise during a nighttime drill the first time anyone has touched it.

Keep a simple record — date, time achieved, and what you found — even just a note on your phone. Compare it every time you redrill. In search-and-rescue, the plans that actually worked were the ones that had been walked and rewalked until they were automatic, not the ones that just looked good on paper; a fire drill in your own home works the same way. The hesitations will disappear, your own numbers will come down, and eventually the whole household will move without needing to be told. That is the actual goal — not a perfect drawing, but a family that doesn't have to think when it matters.

Put the first one on the calendar this March, during Fire Prevention Month, and then put the next one on the calendar too — three months out, six months out, whenever your household changes. Once a year is a start. It is not enough on its own.
