## The situation

Every typhoon season, the same pattern repeats. The rain comes, the esteros overflow, the street outside becomes a brown river, and somewhere in that water is rat urine carrying *Leptospira* bacteria. It gets into the water from rat droppings and urine washed out of drains, gutters, and garbage, and it can get into you through a cut, a scrape, or even just soft, waterlogged skin, or through your eyes, nose, and mouth if you swallow or splash the water.

This is why hospitals across Metro Manila and the provinces see a predictable jump in leptospirosis cases every year after major flooding — after Ondoy, after Ulysses, after every named storm that leaves streets underwater for more than a day. It is not rare, and it is not exotic. It is a known, seasonal risk that comes with living in a flood-prone barangay, and it is manageable if you know what to do.

This guide is about that specific risk — the biological one in the water itself, not the question of whether the water is safe to drink. If you are looking for how to purify water for drinking after a flood, that is a separate topic and worth reading on its own: [clean water](/clean-water/) after a disaster is a different problem with different steps.

## What actually matters

A few things make almost all of the difference, and none of them are complicated:

- **Broken skin is the main door in.** A fresh cut, a healing wound, even a small blister — any break in the skin is an open invitation when it meets contaminated water.
- **Time in the water matters.** A few seconds crossing a flooded street is a different exposure than wading for twenty minutes to check on a neighbor or move furniture.
- **What you do in the first hour after wading matters more than anything you can buy.** Washing off promptly cuts your risk more than any piece of gear.
- **Symptoms show up days later, not immediately.** This catches people off guard — they feel fine on the day, then get sick four to fourteen days after, and don't connect it back to the flood.

Nothing here requires panic. It requires a short list of habits, done consistently, every time floodwater touches your skin.

## What to do (and what not to do)

**Before you wade, if you have any choice in the matter:**
Cover any open cut or wound with a waterproof plaster or dressing first. Wear closed, waterproof footwear — rubber boots, not sandals or bare feet. If you can avoid wading altogether, that is always the better option; no boot or bandage replaces staying out of the water in the first place.

**If you have already waded through floodwater:**
Wash your legs, arms, and any exposed skin with soap and clean water as soon as you reasonably can. This does not need to be elaborate — a bucket bath with soap is enough. Pay particular attention to cuts, scrapes, and the skin around your toenails, where water sits longest. If you got water in your eyes, rinse them with clean water. Change out of wet clothes; do not let mud-caked clothing sit on your skin for hours because it was the only dry-ish thing around.

If you have a wound that was submerged, clean it properly — soap and water, then an antiseptic if you have one, then a clean dry dressing. Watch it over the next few days for signs of infection, and watch yourself for the symptoms below over the next two weeks.

**What not to do:** Don't wait it out hoping symptoms won't come. Don't self-medicate with leftover antibiotics from a previous illness — dosage and drug matter, and getting it wrong can do more harm than good. And don't assume a small, brief wade means you're automatically fine; assume exposure happened, do the wash-down, and just keep an eye on yourself.

**Symptoms to watch for, days 4 to 14 after exposure:**
Sudden fever, chills, headache, and muscle pain — especially in the calves, which is a fairly distinctive sign. Red, irritated eyes without discharge. Sometimes nausea, vomiting, or a rash. Early leptospirosis can look a lot like flu or dengue, which is exactly why the flood history matters — tell whoever treats you that you waded through floodwater in the past two weeks. That one sentence changes how a doctor reads your symptoms.

If fever and muscle pain show up after flood exposure, see a doctor promptly rather than waiting to see if it passes on its own. Leptospirosis responds well to antibiotics started early; it gets much harder to manage once it progresses. Preventive antibiotics after a known heavy exposure are sometimes given, but only under a doctor's judgment — this is not something to arrange for yourself from a pharmacy. If you're unsure where to go, your barangay health center is the first stop, and the <a href="https://doh.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Department of Health</a> publishes current advisories and contact information for its regional offices during typhoon season — worth checking before the water even rises, so you're not searching for it while sick.

## The gear that helps

None of this needs to be expensive, and none of it replaces judgment. A pair of rubber wading boots (roughly ₱300–₱500) is worth more here than almost anything else on this list — they keep floodwater off skin entirely for the most common exposure, which is walking through ankle- or knee-deep water. Waterproof plasters and a small tube of antiseptic (₱50–₱150 combined) mean a cut doesn't have to become an open door. A bar of soap or a small bottle of liquid soap kept with your go-bag costs next to nothing and is the single most useful item on this whole list.

These all belong in a proper [go bag](/go-bag/) and in your household's [flood ready](/flood-ready/) kit, alongside the [first aid](/first-aid/) basics you'd want on hand anyway — dressings, antiseptic, a working thermometer to catch that early fever. None of this is dramatic gear. It's the unglamorous stuff that quietly does its job.

## Common mistakes

The biggest mistake is treating a small wade as nothing — no wash-down, no wound check, no memory of it two weeks later when the fever hits. The second is waiting too long once symptoms appear, hoping it's just fatigue from the storm cleanup. The third is over-relying on boots and forgetting the wound underneath the sock, or the cut on a hand from clearing debris — leptospirosis doesn't only come in through the feet.

## The one thing to remember

Floodwater in the Philippines should be treated as contaminated, full stop, every single time. Wash off after you wade, cover the cuts before and after, and if fever and muscle pain follow a flood within two weeks, tell your doctor about the water — that one detail is often what gets it caught and treated early.
