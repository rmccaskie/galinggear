## What you'll be able to do — and why it matters

When <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> raises a signal and your water district or MWSS concessionaire posts the advisory to "please store enough water," that is not boilerplate. Water districts routinely suspend service once a typhoon makes landfall — sometimes for a few hours, sometimes for two or three days if a treatment plant loses power or intake lines get fouled with silt. I have sat through outages in a fourth-floor unit with no lift running and no water pressure to speak of, doing the math on how many liters were left in the drum by the door.

This guide is about the boring, useful thing you do *before* the wind picks up: filling clean containers with clean water while the taps still work, storing that water so it survives the storm untouched, and knowing how much is actually enough. It will not tell you how to treat floodwater that has already gone bad — that is a different job, with a different set of tools, and I cover it in Floodwater to drinking water: pre-filter, treat, store. This one is about never needing that article in the first place, for as long as your stored supply holds.

## What you need

**Water itself, in the right volume.** The rule of thumb I use is 3 liters per person per day for drinking and cooking, plus another 2 liters per person per day if you want enough for basic hygiene — brushing teeth, sponge baths, washing a few dishes. For a family of four, that is roughly 20 liters a day, or a single food-grade 20-liter drum. Plan for at least three days minimum; five is more realistic if you are in a low-lying barangay where road access can take a while to clear.

**Food-grade containers.** This is the part people get wrong most often. Look for:

- **Food-grade plastic drums (20L)** — widely available at hardware stores (True Value, Ace Hardware, Handyman) and on Shopee or Lazada for around ₱200–₱500. A recycling symbol with a "2" (HDPE) or "1" (PET) on the bottom is a useful clue, but it is not proof by itself — plenty of non-food plastic carries the same codes. What actually matters is an explicit "food grade" or "for potable water" label, or a container you know was manufactured to hold food or drinking water. When in doubt, buy new and labeled rather than guess from the resin code alone.
- **Repurposed mineral water jugs (the round 5-gallon kind used with dispensers)** — fine if they originally held drinking water and you rinse them well. Do not repurpose a jug that held distilled water for a flat iron, or one whose origin you cannot vouch for.
- **What to avoid:** used detergent or bleach containers, old cooking oil containers, and any drum with no markings at all. Plastic that was never meant for food can leach chemicals, and no amount of washing fully removes that risk. If you would not want to guess what was in it, do not put drinking water in it.

**Household bleach for sanitizing (plain, unscented, no additives).** Common Philippine brands like Zonrox and Chlorox regular bleach run about 5% to 6% sodium hypochlorite, and a bottle costs roughly ₱40–₱80 depending on size. Check the label for the percentage before you measure anything — the ratios below assume this 5%–6% range. Avoid anything scented, color-safe, or with added surfactants; you only want the bleach.

**Marker and tape for labelling**, and a cool, elevated, indoor spot to keep everything.

## The method

**1. Watch for the advisory, then act early.**
Local water districts and MWSS often post a notice a day or two ahead of a typhoon's expected landfall, asking households to store water before service is interrupted. Take that notice literally and fill up the same day — do not wait until the wind starts, because pressure can drop before the official cutoff.

**2. Wash every container first.**
Even a brand-new drum can have dust or manufacturing residue inside. Wash with dish soap and rinse thoroughly.

**3. Sanitize the container.**
Make a sanitizing solution of 1 tablespoon of plain 5%–6% bleach per gallon (about 3.8 liters) of water — roughly 1 teaspoon per liter if you are mixing a smaller batch. Pour it into the empty container, cap it, and shake or swirl so the solution reaches every interior surface, including the cap and threads. Let it stand for at least two minutes — longer, up to ten minutes, is fine and safer if you have the time. Pour it out, then rinse thoroughly with clean water until you can no longer smell bleach. Do this for every drum or jug you plan to use for drinking water — not for the ones you are setting aside purely for flushing toilets or washing floors.

**4. Fill with tap water while service is still running.**
Fill drinking-water containers to the brim, cap tightly, and leave no unnecessary air gap. If your tap water comes from a chlorinated MWSS concessionaire or water district supply, it is already treated, and storing it sealed and untouched for a few days does not require you to add more bleach. If you are unsure about your local supply's quality, drawing from a shallow well, or the water looks even slightly cloudy, treat it before storing: add 2 drops of 5%–6% bleach per liter of clear water, or 4 drops per liter if the water is cloudy or you are unsure of its clarity. Stir well and let it stand for 30 minutes before sealing — you should be able to smell a faint chlorine odor at the end of that wait; if you cannot, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. For anything beyond routine household storage of tap water, follow the <a href="https://doh.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Department of Health</a>'s published guidance on household water disinfection rather than relying on a single rule of thumb.

**5. Label everything immediately.**
Use a marker directly on the container or masking tape: "DRINKING" on one batch, "WASH/FLUSH ONLY" on another, and the fill date. Water meant for cleaning, flushing, or washing does not need the same sanitizing rigor, but mixing up which drum is which under stress, in the dark, during a brownout, is a real and avoidable mistake.

**6. Place containers away from flood-prone floors.**
Ground floor units and provincial homes near creeks or esteros should not store water at floor level if that floor has flooded before. Put drums on a table, a sturdy shelf, or an elevated platform. The goal is simple: if floodwater does enter the house, it should not be able to reach the cap and seep in, and the container itself should not float away or tip in moving water. In a multi-story home, an interior room on the second floor is ideal — away from windows, out of direct sun, and away from anything that could fall on the containers during strong wind.

**7. Rotate the reserve even when no storm is coming.**
Stored tap water that sits sealed and undisturbed is generally fine for a few months, but do not treat a full drum as a "set and forget" fixture of the house. Empty, rinse, and refill drinking-water containers roughly every three to six months, and write the fill date on the label so you are not guessing. Stagnant water can pick up odd tastes or bacterial growth over time even in a sealed container, especially in Philippine heat, and rotating it costs you nothing but a few minutes.

## Variations & conditions

**Condo or apartment on a high floor.** You likely will not flood, but you will lose the lift and possibly the water pump if the building's power goes out — many buildings pump water up using electricity, so a citywide brownout during a typhoon can mean no water at any floor, storm or no storm. Store your reserve as if you were in a house; do not assume height protects your tap.

**Provincial home near a river or low-lying barangay.** Store more days than you think you need — access roads can stay impassable for a while after the storm passes, even if your own house is fine. This is also where I would put containers on the highest stable surface available, not just up on a chair, since these areas can see water rise faster and higher than city storm drains.

**If the storm brings a prolonged outage.** Once your stored reserve is gone and you still do not have safe tap water, do not start treating whatever you can find without a plan — that is exactly the situation covered in Floodwater to drinking water: pre-filter, treat, store. Pre-storm storage and post-flood treatment are two different skills for two different phases of the same event, and confusing them is how people end up drinking something they shouldn't.

## Common mistakes

**Waiting until the signal is raised to start filling containers.** By then pressure may already be dropping, and hardware stores selling drums will be picked over.

**Using a container that once held something non-food.** Even faint residue from bleach, oil, or paint can taint water enough to make it undrinkable or worse.

**Trusting the recycling code alone.** A "1" or "2" stamp tells you the plastic type, not whether the container was ever meant to touch food or water. Look for explicit food-grade labeling instead.

**Under-measuring or rushing the sanitizing step.** A quick 30-second swirl with too little bleach is not the same as the full tablespoon-per-gallon mix left standing for at least two minutes. Rushing this step defeats the point of doing it at all.

**Forgetting to label, and mixing up drinking and washing water days later.** In a brownout with the whole family stressed and moving around by flashlight, an unlabeled drum is a genuine hazard, not just an inconvenience.

**Storing water at floor level in a home that has flooded before.** I have seen a family lose an entire reserve because the drums were sitting exactly where the water came in.

**Letting stored water sit for a year without rotating it.** Out of sight is not the same as safe. A drum that has not been touched since last typhoon season should be emptied, rinsed, and refilled before you trust it again.

**Assuming a full drum means you are done.** Water alone does not cover food, meds, light, or communication — it is one part of a broader [go bag](/go-bag/) and [flood ready](/flood-ready/) plan, not the whole plan.

## How to practise safely

Do a dry run before typhoon season peaks — usually well before June, so you are not scrambling in the middle of an active <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> bulletin. Buy your drums now while they are easy to find, sanitize them once, and keep them clean and dry until you actually need to fill them. Practice the bleach measurements once so they are not a fumbled guess later — a tablespoon per gallon for sanitizing an empty container, a couple of drops per liter for treating water you are unsure of — because getting these wrong in either direction either wastes bleach or leaves the container under-treated. Walk your own house and decide, ahead of time, exactly which shelf or table each drum will sit on when the advisory comes. Mark your calendar for a rotation check every few months, storm season or not. The best time to solve "where does this go" is a calm afternoon, not the hour before a storm makes landfall.

No amount of stored water replaces leaving early if local officials call for evacuation — a full drum is a comfort during an outage, not a reason to stay somewhere that floods.
