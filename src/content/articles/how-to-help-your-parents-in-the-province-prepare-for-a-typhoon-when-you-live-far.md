## What you'll be able to do — and why it matters

By the end of this, you should be able to sit down with your phone on a quiet Sunday and, in about two hours spread over a week, put your parents in a much better position for the next storm — without being there.

That means: a short plan they have actually agreed to, a box of supplies that has arrived and been unpacked, medicines refilled ahead of time, three phone numbers saved in your phone and in theirs, a known evacuation centre, and an agreed check-in rhythm with a fallback for when signal drops.

What it does not mean is control. You cannot evacuate someone by phone from Dubai. You cannot make an eighty-year-old leave a house he built himself. What you can do is remove every obstacle except the decision, and make the decision easier to say yes to. That is a real amount of work and it is worth doing.

The hardest part is not logistics. It is that "okay lang kami" is not a status report. It is love, and pride, and not wanting to be a burden to a child who is already working far from home. Take it as affection, not as information, and keep asking gently.

## What you need

Nothing here is gear you have to buy first. Most of it is information.

**Numbers, saved properly.** Your parents' mobile numbers on both networks if they have two SIMs. The barangay captain or the barangay hall. One trusted neighbour — ideally someone under sixty who lives within shouting distance. A cousin or relative in the same town. Save them with clear names: "Barangay Captain — Tatay's barangay", not "Kap". You will be looking for these at two in the morning.

**A source of weather truth you both use.** Agree on one. <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> is the source; the rest is commentary. If you both look at the same bulletin, you stop arguing about whose Facebook post is right. Teach them the signal numbers if they are not already fluent — many older folks are more fluent than we are.

**A short supply list, not a survival kit.** Two weeks of maintenance medicines. Drinking water. Rice, tinned fish, coffee, sugar, biscuits. A torch each with spare batteries — not one shared torch. A small radio that runs on batteries. Candles are what they will reach for; a torch is what you want them to reach for, so buy good ones and make them easy to find. A power bank, charged. Rope or nylon cord. A tarpaulin. Cash in small notes, because when power is out, GCash and card terminals are out too.

**A local ally.** This is the single most valuable item on the list and it costs nothing. One neighbour who will physically knock on the door.

**A shared plan with the rest of the family.** If you have siblings, decide now who calls, who sends money, who books the ride home. Our [family communication plan guide](/stay-connected/) covers the mechanics of that — who calls whom, in what order, and what happens when the network is gone.

## The method

**Step one: have the conversation before the season, not during.** March to May is the time. Nobody is frightened, nothing is urgent, and you can talk about it as planning rather than as panic. Once there is a storm in the Philippine Area of Responsibility, every conversation becomes an argument.

**Step two: agree the trigger, not the outcome.** Do not start with "you have to evacuate". Start with "at what point would you go?" Let them set it. Most people will say something reasonable — Signal No. 3, or water reaching the road, or the barangay coming round with the megaphone. Write down what they say and read it back. You now have a plan in their words, which is the only kind they will follow.

**Step three: find the evacuation centre and how they get there.** Usually the elementary school, the covered court or the barangay hall. Call the barangay and confirm — assignments change. Then ask the harder question: how do they physically get there? Who drives? Is there a tricycle at 4am in the rain? If the answer is "we will walk", and Nanay is seventy-eight with a bad knee, that is the problem you need to solve now, not at 4am.

**Step four: order supplies early and confirm they landed.** Lazada and Shopee both deliver to most provincial addresses, though the courier list thins out the further you go and delivery times stretch. Order in May or June, not in the week a storm is named. Expect ₱1,500 to ₱3,000 to cover water containers, torches, batteries, a radio and a power bank, plus food money sent separately.

Then do the part everyone skips: confirm delivery by video call. Not "did it arrive?" — "can you show me?" I have had a torch sit in its box on top of a cabinet for eight months because nobody opened it. Get them to unwrap it, put the batteries in, switch it on, and put it where they will actually look for it. Cheap counterfeit batteries and no-name power banks are common; buy from the bigger official stores and expect to pay a bit more.

**Step five: refill medicines early.** This is the one I would do first if I could only do one thing. Maintenance medicines for blood pressure, diabetes, heart — get two weeks ahead of the usual refill date before the season starts, and keep it there. After a storm, pharmacies close, roads are cut and the delivery trucks do not come for days. Ask which medicines, write the actual names and doses down in your own notes, and photograph the boxes. If they end up in an evacuation centre or a hospital, you will be asked and they may not remember.

Keep a plastic envelope by the door with photocopies: <a href="https://www.philhealth.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PhilHealth</a> ID, senior citizen ID, a list of medicines and allergies, and the doctor's number. It weighs nothing and it saves an hour at the worst possible time.

**Step six: agree the check-in.** One fixed time a day during a storm — say 7am and 7pm. Short calls. Text is better than voice when the network is congested; a text will often squeeze through when a call will not.

Then agree the fallback, and be specific: *if you do not hear from them within three hours of the agreed time, you call the neighbour; if the neighbour does not answer, you call the barangay.* Say this out loud to your parents so they know a silence will trigger a phone ringing next door. Some of them will start answering just to spare the neighbour the trouble. That is fine.

**Step seven: send the one-pager.** Print it, or send it as a photo they can keep on the fridge. It is below.

## Variations and conditions

**If you are abroad, mind the time difference.** Set the check-in to a time that works in both places, and tell your boss in advance during storm season. Scrambling for permission to take a call is the last thing you need.

**If they live in a low-lying or coastal barangay,** storm surge and flooding matter more than wind, and the timeline is shorter. Look up their exact address on <a href="https://hazardhunter.georisk.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">HazardHunterPH</a> once, calmly, so you know what you are actually dealing with — flood, landslide, or neither. It takes five minutes and it will change how hard you push.

**If they live alone on a high floor in the city** — different problem, same principle. The lift goes with the power and the water pump usually goes with it too. Stored water and a torch on every floor of the house matter more than a go bag.

**If the house is solid concrete on high ground,** staying may genuinely be the right call, and your parents may be right. Listen. The plan is still the same: supplies, medicines, check-ins, a neighbour.

**When the barangay says go, that overrides everything,** including your plan and their preference. Local officials know which creek backs up first. If there is a forced evacuation order, the conversation is over — and it helps enormously if you have already said, months earlier, "if the barangay says go, you go, and I will not argue with you about the house."

## Common mistakes

**Calling only when there is a storm.** If your voice only appears when there is bad news, they start softening reports to protect you. Call on ordinary Tuesdays. The storm calls will go better.

**Sending money instead of arranging things.** Money is welcome and it is not a plan. A ₱5,000 padala the day before landfall buys nothing if the shops are shuttered and the ATM queue is an hour long.

**Buying gear nobody has touched.** In search-and-rescue we had a hard rule: you do not carry a piece of kit into the field that you have not used in daylight, in the dry, with your own hands. The same rule applies to a torch on a shelf in Bicol. Unopened gear is not gear.

**Arguing about evacuation during the storm.** That argument is lost before it starts. Have it in April.

**Nagging.** Repeating the same warning louder does not increase compliance, it just teaches them to change the subject. Ask questions instead of issuing instructions. "What would make you decide to go?" gets further than "you have to go."

**Forgetting the neighbour.** The most useful person in this entire plan lives thirty metres from your parents and can be at their door in under a minute. Get their number. Send them something at Christmas.

## The script

Use your own words, but this is the shape of it. Keep it short and keep it light.

> "Ma, Pa — before the rainy season, can I ask you a few things? Hindi ako nag-aalala, gusto ko lang malinaw sa akin."
>
> "If there's a strong typhoon, saan kayo pupunta? Is it still the elementary school?"
>
> "At what point would you decide to go? Signal 3? Kapag umabot na ang tubig sa kalsada? I just want to know what you're watching for, so when I call I'll know what to ask."
>
> "Who's the nearest neighbour I can call if I can't reach you? Can I get their number, and can you tell them I might call? Baka matakot sila kung biglang tatawag ang taga-Manila."
>
> "Can you show me your medicines on video? I want to write the names down para alam ko."
>
> "I'll send a few things for the house — torches, batteries, a radio. Pag dumating, can we open them together on video call? Gusto ko lang masigurado na gumagana."
>
> "During a storm let's text at 7 in the morning and 7 at night, kahit isang linya lang. If I don't hear from you by 10, I'll call Aling Ising. Not because I'm panicking — para lang hindi ako mabaliw dito."
>
> "And Pa — if the barangay says lumikas na, please go. I'm not going to argue with you about the house. Kaya nating ayusin ang bahay. Ikaw, hindi."

That last line is the one that matters, and it lands much better said in April than at midnight with the roof going.

## How to practise safely

A plan you have never run is a guess.

**Do a dry run before the season.** Pick a random Saturday. Send the 7am text. See if it comes back. Call the neighbour's number to check it still works — numbers change constantly. Ring the barangay hall and confirm the evacuation centre. Fifteen minutes, once a year.

**Do a torch drill.** Ask them to find their torch while you are on the phone. Time it. If it takes more than thirty seconds, it is in the wrong place.

**Check the supply box in May.** Batteries corrode, biscuits expire, water stored in old soft drink bottles goes off. Make it an annual thing, tied to a date you will remember.

**Practise the medicine refill rhythm** until being two weeks ahead is simply normal, not a storm measure.

**Walk the route yourself when you visit.** Next time you are home, walk from the house to the evacuation centre. Notice the low spot on the road, the bridge, the bit that turns to mud. You will understand their hesitation much better, and your advice will stop sounding like it came from someone who has not been home in three years.

And hold this lightly. You will do everything on this list and they will still stay in the house one time when you wish they had not. That is their right, and they have lived through more storms than you have. Your job is not to win. It is to make sure that if they change their mind at 2am, everything they need is by the door, the neighbour is awake, and someone knows to look for them.

## The one-page checklist

Print this, photograph it, send it. Stick it on the fridge.

**Numbers by the phone**
- Parents' mobile (both networks)
- Neighbour: ________________
- Barangay hall / captain: ________________
- Relative in town: ________________
- Doctor / health centre: ________________

**Before the season (March–May)**
- [ ] Agreed evacuation trigger, in their words: ________________
- [ ] Confirmed evacuation centre: ________________
- [ ] Confirmed how they get there, and who takes them
- [ ] Medicines two weeks ahead of refill date
- [ ] Photos of medicine boxes saved on my phone
- [ ] Plastic envelope by the door: PhilHealth ID, senior ID, medicine list, doctor's number

**Supplies delivered and opened on video call**
- [ ] Torch each, plus spare batteries
- [ ] Battery radio
- [ ] Power bank, charged
- [ ] Water containers filled
- [ ] Two weeks of food they actually eat
- [ ] Cash in small notes
- [ ] Rope and tarpaulin

**When a storm is named**
- [ ] Both watching PAGASA bulletins, not rumours
- [ ] Phones and power bank fully charged
- [ ] Water containers refilled
- [ ] Go bag by the door; documents in it
- [ ] Neighbour told I may call
- [ ] Check-in times confirmed: 7am / 7pm
- [ ] Fallback agreed: no reply by 10am → call neighbour → call barangay

**Always**
- If the barangay says evacuate, they evacuate. No debate, no exceptions.

We buy our own gear and we pay our own way, including the boxes we send home. No bag, no torch and no checklist replaces leaving early — but a plan agreed in April makes leaving early a great deal more likely.
