## Overview

I have owned this headlamp long enough to forget I own it, which is the highest compliment I can pay a piece of kit. The Nitecore NU25 is a small rechargeable headlamp, about the weight of two ten-peso coins with the band on, and it has ridden in the lid pocket of my day bag through two wet seasons, three long brownouts and one very unglamorous evening under a sink.

It is here for one reason: it is light enough that you will actually carry it, and bright enough that you will not wish you had brought something else.

## Why it matters in Philippine conditions

Our power does not usually fail at a convenient hour. It fails while the rice is on, or at half past nine when the kids are already in bed and PAGASA has just upgraded the signal for your area. If you live on a high floor and the lift goes with the grid, you are walking down a concrete stairwell with no windows, holding something in one hand and a child's hand in the other.

That is the case for a headlamp over a torch, and I do not think it is close. Hands free means you can hold the railing, carry the bag, or open the breaker box and still see the screws. A phone torch does the job for about ninety seconds before you realise you have just spent your remaining battery lighting a hallway.

The other local reality is damp. Nothing in a Philippine go-bag stays dry by accident. The NU25 has shrugged off being worn in the rain and being dropped on wet tiles, and I will be precise about its limits further down.

## Key specs: what you get

- **Weight:** around 45 g with the headband on the current USB-C version — light enough that you forget it is on your head
- **Maximum output:** 400 lumens on the highest setting (the older micro-USB model is 360 lumens)
- **Light sources:** a white spotlight for reach, a white floodlight for close work, and a red light for night vision and for not blinding the person next to you
- **Battery:** built-in 650 mAh lithium cell, charged by USB-C, **not** user-replaceable
- **Charging:** roughly two hours from flat off a phone charger or a power bank
- **Water resistance:** IP66 — in plain terms, it will take heavy rain and hosing, but it is not made for being dropped in flood water
- **Beam distance:** a little under 80 metres on the brightest setting
- **Runtime:** minutes on the top setting, hours on the middle settings, and days on the lowest — Nitecore quote up to 160 hours on the dimmest white

Two of those numbers deserve translating. "400 lumens" is plenty to walk a dark barangay road or find the water shut-off, but it is not a search beam, and the headlamp will not hold 400 lumens for long — it steps down as heat and battery allow. And "160 hours" is on a setting so dim it is really a nightlight. Both figures are honest; just know which one you are actually using.

## How it performs


<figure class="gg-figure"><img class="gg-zoomable" src="https://images.galinggear.com/articles/sourced-1789734462417-tmdf9b.jpg" alt="Nitecore NU25 review: the headlamp I stopped thinking about" loading="lazy" /></figure>


The setting I use most is not the bright one. It is the second or third step down, somewhere around 20 to 40 lumens, and that is where this headlamp lives most of its life: reading a meter, checking a leaking window seal, sorting the medicine pouch at two in the morning. On that sort of setting I have gone through a whole evening outage and still had charge left the next day.

The floodlight is the one I reach for indoors. It is a soft, wide pool of light with no harsh hotspot, which matters more than it sounds — a spot beam in a small room is tiring and makes everything outside the circle look darker than it is. Outdoors, in rain, the spot is the right tool. Water in the air throws light back at you, and a wider beam just lights up the drizzle.

The red light earns its keep in a crowded room. During one three-day outage in our building, the red mode was what let me move around without waking anyone, and it is the setting I would put a child on if they needed a light of their own.

The single-button interface is fine once learned and mildly annoying before that. A short press cycles brightness, a long press switches light sources, and there is a lock so it does not turn itself on inside your bag. I would call the lock a feature and not a nicety: a headlamp that quietly drains itself in a side pocket is a headlamp that is not there when you need it.

The clip and band are simple and have not slipped or perished. Mine has lived in a bag that is regularly damp, and nothing has corroded.

Now the fault. The battery is sealed in. In search-and-rescue we always carried one light that ran on swappable cells, because a sealed battery is a single point of failure — when it is flat and there is no power, you have a paperweight, and no amount of cleverness gets around that at three in the morning. The NU25 answers it well enough if you also carry a power bank, which you should anyway, but it should shape how you build your kit rather than being glossed over. This is not the only light you own.

IP66 is the second limit worth stating plainly. Rain, yes. Spray off a roof, yes. A dunk in flood water in a low-lying street, no — and flood water is the last thing you want getting into a sealed electronics housing you cannot open.

## Pros and cons

**Pros**

- Light enough to carry every day, which is the whole point
- Genuinely useful spread of modes: flood for close work, spot for distance, red for keeping the peace
- USB-C charging off the same power bank you already keep charged
- Lock-out mode stops accidental drain in a bag
- Low settings sip power and run for days

**Cons**

- Non-replaceable battery — no swapping in AAAs when it dies
- IP66 is rain-proof, not flood-proof
- Top output does not hold for long before stepping down
- Single-button interface takes a week to become second nature
- Widely counterfeited online, so where you buy matters

## Who it's for

The person who wants one light they will actually have on them. Commuters, anyone in a mid-rise or high-rise where an outage means stairs, parents assembling a go-bag who need something small enough that it does not compete for space with water and medicine. If your kit has drifted towards heavy things you never carry, this is the correction.

It is also a sensible second light for hikers and for anyone doing a bit of off-grid cooking or camp work — you can clip it to a cap brim and work with both hands.

## Who it's not for

If you want one light to cover everything, including long outages with no way to recharge, this is not it on its own. Buy a headlamp that takes AA or AAA cells for that job, or pair this one with a power bank and accept that you are now carrying two things.

If you work in water — fisherfolk, anyone who expects to be wading rather than walking — look for a properly submersible light instead. And if you need to throw a beam 200 metres across a field, this is the wrong size of tool entirely.

## Budget alternative

If ₱2,000-plus is not in the budget this month, a plain 3×AAA headlamp from a hardware store at ₱350 to ₱700 will get you through a brownout. It will be heavier, the beam will be uglier, and the plastic will not last as long — but it takes cells you can buy at the sari-sari store, which is a real advantage. Honestly, many households should own both.

## The verdict and score

This is not exciting gear and it is not pretending to be. It is a small, well-made headlamp that has quietly done its job every time I have asked, weighs so little that the decision to carry it takes no thought, and gives you red light and flood light as well as a beam. The sealed battery is a genuine limitation and the reason it is not a five, but it is a limitation you can plan around in an afternoon.

If you buy one thing to make the next outage less miserable, this is a strong candidate.

**4.5 / 5**
