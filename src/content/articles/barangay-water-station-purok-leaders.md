Two days after the water went brown in our line, I stood in a barangay hall watching six households line up with whatever containers they had — a *timba*, a soda bottle, an old paint pail rinsed out with dish soap. Nobody had told them yet whether the tap was safe. Nobody could, honestly, because the water district hadn't given the all-clear either. That gap — between the storm passing and the official word coming down — is exactly when a purok-level water station earns its keep.

This guide is for that gap. It is not a permanent fix, and it will not replace a proper water system or a water district's all-clear. Think of it as a bridge: a way to get a few days of safe drinking water to a cluster of households using cloth, sand, chlorine or boiling, and containers you can round up on short notice. When the water district or your local health office issues its own clearance, stand the station down and let the regular system take back over. That decision is about the water district's word, not the weather — a typhoon signal can be lifted by <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> while pipes are still contaminated, so don't treat "signal down" as "tap safe." Wait for the water district or your local health office to say so directly.

## What you'll be able to do — and why it matters

By the end of this, a small team of two to four volunteers should be able to set up a simple community water point that takes in suspect water (floodwater, or tap water of unknown safety), removes visible dirt, treats it to a known standard, and hands it out in a way that does not quietly recontaminate it on the way home.

Why this matters: after a major typhoon, floodwater can get into municipal pipes through cracked joints, low water pressure that lets drainage or groundwater seep in, or submerged pumping stations. This kind of contamination doesn't always clear the moment the rain stops — it can persist for days while the water district flushes and tests the lines. During that window, LGUs and water districts commonly advise residents to treat all tap water as unsafe until they announce otherwise, which is exactly the kind of advisory the <a href="https://doh.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Department of Health</a> also echoes after major flooding events. Your safest default, as a purok leader, is the same: assume every drum, tanker delivery, or tap trickle that comes into your station is contaminated until you have treated it yourself. Treat that as the starting rule, not a footnote.

A water station also has a failure mode nobody likes to talk about: the station itself becomes the source of illness, because a shared dipper touched someone's hand, or a "treated" drum sat uncovered next to a "raw" one and got mixed up. Half of this guide is about treatment. The other half is about keeping the station honest.

## What you need

You will not have engineering-grade equipment, and you don't need it. Work with what a barangay can realistically gather:

- **A site**: covered or shaded, with a clean floor or platform, and somewhere for wastewater and spillage to drain away from the intake area — not toward it.
- **Pre-filter materials**: clean cloth (cotton bedsheet or sack cloth works), and if available, a simple layered sand-and-gravel filter (large stone, then gravel, then sand, then a top cloth layer) built inside a drum or bucket with holes.
- **Storage containers**: food-grade drums or jerry cans with lids, ideally ones that have not been used for chemicals or fuel. Clearly mark which are for raw (untreated) water and which are for treated water — different color lids or bold marker labels work.
- **Treatment supplies**: a source of clean fuel and a pot big enough to boil in batches, and/or plain unscented household chlorine bleach (5% to 6% sodium hypochlorite, no added scent, soap, or color-safe formula) or water purification tablets intended for drinking water. If the product has a label, follow it exactly — labels vary by concentration and brand. If you have no label, use the fallback dosage in Step 2, which matches <a href="https://doh.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">DOH</a> and <a href="https://redcross.org.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Philippine Red Cross</a> emergency water disinfection guidance.
- **Dedicated dippers or ladles** — one per drum, never shared bare hands or personal cups touching the water inside a communal container.
- **Handwashing station**: a tippy-tap, a jerry can with a tap, or even a pitcher-and-basin setup with soap, placed at the entrance to the line.
- **Simple log**: a notebook or even a sheet taped to the wall, to record batch times and who collected what.
- **Signage materials**: masking tape, marker, cardboard — enough to make "SAFE TO DRINK," "FOR WASHING ONLY," and time/date labels.

None of this needs to be fancy. A tarp-covered table, three drums, and a kettle can be a functioning water station if the steps below are followed in order.

## The method

### Step 1: Choose the site and set up the pre-filter

1. Pick a spot on slightly higher ground, away from latrines, garbage, and standing floodwater, with a roof or tarp overhead if possible.
2. Make sure spilled or waste water drains away from the station, not back toward where you're collecting or storing water.
3. If incoming water is heavily silted or muddy, let it settle in a covered container for at least 30 minutes to an hour first, then pour off the clearer water on top, leaving the settled sediment behind. This single step saves your cloth and sand filters from clogging within the first hour.
4. Set up your pre-filter before anything else touches the water. At its simplest, this is folded cloth stretched over the mouth of a drum to strain out sediment, leaves, and debris. If you have the materials and time, build a layered sand-and-gravel filter in a second drum — this removes more sediment but does **not** make water safe to drink on its own. It only prepares it for treatment.
5. Set up an intake table where households pour their raw water through the pre-filter into a marked "RAW / UNTREATED" drum. This drum never touches the treated side of the station.

### Step 2: Treat every batch, and confirm it visibly

6. Treat all incoming water as contaminated by default — this includes tap water, unless your water district or LGU has already issued an all-clear for your area.
7. Choose your treatment method based on what you have:
   - **Boiling**: bring the water to a full rolling boil — big bubbles breaking the surface continuously, not just simmering — and keep it boiling for at least **1 full minute** at normal lowland elevation. If your barangay sits above roughly 2,000 meters elevation, extend that to **3 minutes**, since water boils at a lower temperature up there. Let it cool in a covered container before transferring.
   - **Chlorine (household bleach)**: use plain, unscented household bleach at 5% sodium hypochlorite, with no soap, color-safe additive, or added scent. If there's a product label, follow it exactly. If there is no label, use this standard emergency dosage: **8 drops (about 0.4 mL, roughly 1/8 teaspoon) of 5% bleach per liter of clear water**. If the water is cloudy even after settling and pre-filtering, double it to about **16 drops per liter**. Stir well.
   - **Purification tablets**: follow the exact dose printed on the package for the stated volume — tablet strength varies by brand, and there is no safe generic substitute for a missing label.
8. Treat in clearly sized batches (per-drum, not per-cup) so you always know exactly what has and hasn't been treated.
9. After treatment, let the batch stand, covered, for at least **30 minutes** before anyone drinks it if you used chlorine or tablets — this contact time is what actually kills the pathogens, not the dose alone. Boiled water is safe to drink once it has cooled; it needs no additional wait. If a product label specifies a longer contact time, follow the label instead.
10. The moment a batch is confirmed treated, pour or transfer it into a separate, marked "TREATED / SAFE TO DRINK" container. Write the date and time of treatment on a piece of tape stuck to that drum, and log it in your notebook.
11. Never top up a treated drum with untreated water. When a treated drum runs low, finish it, rinse it if needed, and start a fresh treated batch — don't mix old and new.

### Step 3: Distribute fairly and label honestly

12. Decide on a simple ration per household (for example, a fixed number of liters per family per day) based on how much treated water you can realistically produce — better to under-promise and be consistent than run out mid-line.
13. Set a collection schedule if the line is long, so people aren't standing around all day, and post it where everyone can see it.
14. Keep a visible, separate area and drum for non-potable water — water that's been filtered for washing or cleaning but not treated for drinking. Label it clearly as "FOR WASHING/CLEANING ONLY — NOT FOR DRINKING." This prevents someone grabbing the wrong drum in a hurry.
15. Log each household's collection in your notebook: name, amount, and time. This isn't bureaucracy for its own sake — it lets you spot if supply is running short before the drum is empty.

### Step 4: Protect the water with basic hygiene discipline

16. Put your handwashing station right at the front of the line and ask everyone to use it before they get near the treated drum.
17. Use one dedicated dipper or ladle per drum — never let bare hands, personal cups, or bottles dip directly into a communal container. Even clean-looking hands carry germs that can spoil an entire batch.
18. Keep any volunteer who currently has diarrhea, vomiting, an open wound, or a skin infection on their hands away from direct water handling duties — assign them to logging, queue management, or signage instead. This is a standard food- and water-safety precaution, not a judgment on the person.
19. Have households present their own containers to be filled by a volunteer using the dipper, rather than letting people help themselves. This slows the line slightly but keeps control of what touches the water.
20. Ask households to keep their filled containers covered on the way home — an open pail on a tricycle ride home undoes a lot of the earlier work.
21. Rotate or wash the dippers and ladles regularly, and keep them off the ground when not in use — hang them or rest them in a clean container.

### Step 5: Store treated water properly

22. Store treated water in food-grade containers with tight, secure lids — never in containers that previously held chemicals, fuel, or agricultural products.
23. Keep containers off the ground, on a pallet, table, or raised platform, to avoid contamination from splash-back, insects, or floodwater residue.
24. Keep storage out of direct sunlight where possible. Heat speeds up chlorine loss and can soften or weaken some plastic containers over days of exposure — it's not an overnight problem, but a station running for several days in full sun will see its chlorine residual fade faster than one kept shaded.
25. Physically separate treated drinking water storage from non-potable washing water storage — different corner of the station, different color coding, no ambiguity.
26. As a conservative default, use chlorine-treated water within **24 hours** of treatment, and boiled water within the same day it was boiled, once it's been opened or is being dispensed from a communal drum. Sealed, untouched containers may hold safely a little longer, but a drum being dipped into repeatedly by a station in daily use should be treated as good for one day only. When in doubt, treat a fresh batch rather than serve an old one — this is cheap insurance against a slow buildup of contamination in a drum that gets opened dozens of times a day.

## Variations & conditions

**If you have no chlorine and no fuel to boil with:** a sand-and-gravel pre-filter alone is not enough — it removes sediment, not pathogens. In this situation, prioritize getting purification tablets or coordinate with your city or municipal disaster office, <a href="https://redcross.org.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Red Cross</a> chapter, or <a href="https://doh.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">DOH</a> for emergency supply rather than distributing filtered-only water as "safe to drink." Label it honestly as "filtered, not disinfected" if that's genuinely all you can offer, and be clear with households about the limits.

**If floodwater is still receding around the site:** move the station to higher ground even if it's less convenient. A station that gets reflooded loses everything — your treated drums, your labels, your log — and can actively spread contamination once floodwater mixes back in.

**At night or with limited lighting:** keep the raw and treated sides of the station physically far apart and use differently shaped or colored containers, not just labels, since labels are harder to read in the dark and volunteers get tired. Battery lanterns at the intake and distribution points reduce mix-ups more than most people expect.

**If demand outpaces your treatment capacity:** shorten the ration rather than skip the contact time or dosage instructions on your treatment method. A smaller amount of properly treated water is safer than a larger amount rushed through.

## Common mistakes — and how to avoid them

- **Assuming tap water is fine because it's "coming from the line, not the flood."** Pipes lose pressure and pick up cross-connections during and after storms. Treat it as suspect until your water district or LGU confirms otherwise — and remember that a lifted <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> storm signal has nothing to do with whether the water district has finished flushing and testing the lines.
- **Letting one drum serve as both intake and storage.** Water that's been dipped into by unwashed hands, or splashed with raw water, is no longer treated water, even if it started that way.
- **Skipping contact time because the line is long.** Thirty minutes after dosing with chlorine, or a full minute of rolling boil, is not optional — cutting it short defeats the purpose of treating at all.
- **Guessing at chlorine dosage "by feel."** Too little and it doesn't work; too much and you're handing out water that tastes and smells strongly of bleach, which pushes people back toward untreated sources. Measure the drops.
- **No log, no labels.** Without a date and time on each batch, you can't tell an old batch from a fresh one, and you can't spot when supply or quality is slipping.
- **Sharing one cup or dipper across the whole station.** This is the single fastest way for a water station to become the thing that makes people sick, instead of the thing that keeps them well.
- **Storing treated water on bare ground or in the sun.** It looks fine and it usually is — until it isn't. Off the ground, under shade, lid on, every time.
- **Letting a sick volunteer handle the treated side.** Someone with diarrhea or an open wound on their hands can undo a well-run station in one shift, no matter how careful they think they're being.

## How to practise safely

Set this up once during calm weather, before you ever need it under pressure — a dry run in your barangay hall or covered court, using ordinary tap water you know is safe, so you're testing the *system*, not the water. Time how long it takes your team to build the pre-filter, measure a chlorine dose or hit a rolling boil, and process the first ten households through the line. You'll find the bottlenecks quickly — usually it's the handwashing station or the dipper discipline that slows everyone down, and that's exactly the part worth practicing until it's automatic.

Practice measuring the fallback bleach dose too — 8 drops per liter from an actual dropper or syringe, not by eye — so the number is muscle memory rather than something someone has to work out mid-typhoon. Walk your team through the labeling and logging steps until they don't need to think about them, because after a typhoon, tired volunteers skip the steps that feel like paperwork first. The households you're serving won't be able to tell a well-run station from a sloppy one just by looking at it — the difference shows up days later, in whether anyone gets sick. Practice is how you make sure it doesn't.

No water station replaces the water district's own repair and testing work, and it isn't meant to run for weeks. It buys your purok a few safer days while the real system gets back on its feet — and that's worth doing properly.
