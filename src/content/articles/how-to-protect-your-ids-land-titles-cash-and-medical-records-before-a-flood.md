## What you'll be able to do — and why it matters

After the water goes down, the mess that takes the longest to clean up usually isn't the mud. It's the paperwork. A land title soaked into pulp, a birth certificate you need for a <a href="https://www.philhealth.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PhilHealth</a> claim, a passport that has to be replaced before a flight already booked — these are the things that turn a bad week into a bad year.

This guide is about a habit, not a product. By the end of it, you'll have a system where your family's essential documents can survive a flood inside your house, and can leave the house with you in under a minute if you have to evacuate. No bag replaces leaving early when <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> or your barangay calls for it — but if the water comes into your home while you're still inside, your documents shouldn't be the thing you're wading back in for.

## What you need

You don't need much, and you don't need to spend a lot. What matters is that each layer does one job well.

- **A grab-and-go document pouch.** A dedicated, clearly labeled folder or zippered pouch that holds only originals you'd need in the first 72 hours: IDs, land title or condominium certificate, insurance policies, birth/marriage certificates, and a short medication list. Keep it near your go bag, not buried in a cabinet. If you've built a [go bag](/go-bag/) already, this pouch lives right beside it.
- **A proper dry bag, not a sandwich bag.** Roll-top dry bags (5 to 10 liters is plenty for documents) are inexpensive on Lazada or Shopee, usually ₱250 to ₱600 for a decent one. Avoid the ultra-cheap unbranded ones with a single fold-over closure — they leak at the seams under real submersion, and a land title face-down in floodwater for an hour is as good as gone.
- **Laminating sleeves or a home laminator** for documents you use often and can't afford to have degrade from handling: vaccination cards, <a href="https://www.philhealth.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PhilHealth</a> ID, a laminated copy (not the original) of your land title's front page for quick reference. A basic laminator runs about ₱800 to ₱1,500 and pays for itself the first typhoon season.
- **A small fireproof/waterproof document box** for the things that don't need to travel but do need to survive if the house floods while you're at work — original land titles, deeds, and insurance contracts you rarely touch. These run roughly ₱1,200 to ₱3,000 depending on size.
- **A cloud backup you actually control.** A phone scanning app (or just your phone camera) and a personal cloud account — Google Drive, a private email-to-self, or a password-protected folder. This is your copy of last resort, not your only copy.
- **Small bills of cash, sealed separately.** Floods take out ATMs and card terminals fast. A modest reserve in a sealed bag inside the pouch matters more than the documents on the first night.

## The method — step by step

**1. Sort your documents into three tiers.**
Tier one: things you need immediately if you evacuate (IDs, cash, medication list, a photocopy of your land title). Tier two: originals that must survive but don't need to travel (the actual land title, insurance policies, deeds). Tier three: everything else that's useful but replaceable with effort (old receipts, warranties).

**2. Digitize before you organize.**
Scan or photograph every document flat, in good light, both sides. Do this now, while it's dry and unhurried — not during a signal-two typhoon warning. Upload to your cloud account in a folder named clearly by family member. Email yourself a copy too, as a second, independent trail.

**3. Build the grab-and-go pouch.**
Put tier-one originals plus a photocopy of tier-two documents into the labeled pouch. Seal it inside a small dry bag. Place it where every adult in the house knows to find it — next to the go bag, not in a drawer only one person remembers.

**4. Store tier-two originals in the fireproof/waterproof box, elevated.**
In search-and-rescue work, we learned never to trust a single point of failure — the gear that mattered had a backup that didn't depend on the first one working. Apply the same thinking here: the box protects against water and fire, the cloud copy protects against the box itself being lost, and the two don't share a single point of failure. Keep the box on a shelf or cabinet top, not on the floor — even homes that don't fully flood often get ankle-deep water on the ground level.

**5. Laminate what you handle often.**
Vaccination records, <a href="https://www.philhealth.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PhilHealth</a> cards, and anything that gets pulled out for errands should be laminated copies, with the true original stored safely. Never laminate the only original of a legal document — lamination is permanent and some agencies won't accept a laminated original for re-issuance.

**6. Set a six-month refresh habit.**
Re-scan anything new (updated insurance, new medical records) and check the dry bag for wear. Do this alongside your go-bag check, ideally at the start of the wet season.

## Variations & conditions

**High-rise vs. ground floor.** If you're in a Quezon City condo on an upper floor, your risk is less about the water reaching your unit and more about losing the lift during an outage while trying to evacuate with a heavy box. Keep tier-one light and portable; leave the fireproof box in the unit.

**Low-lying and riverside barangays.** If you're near the Marikina River or another flood-prone waterway, treat the fireproof box as the exception, not the rule — for these households, more documents should live in tier one, and the box should be on the highest shelf you have, not just off the floor.

**Rented homes.** If you don't own a fireproof box or safe, a second dry bag stored at a relative's house outside the flood zone works as a physical backup, alongside your cloud copy.

**No internet access.** If your barangay has patchy signal during storms, digitize before the season starts, not during it. <a href="https://asti.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">DOST-ASTI</a> and local government advisories are worth following for connectivity updates, but don't depend on uploading anything mid-emergency.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting a sandwich bag or grocery bag as a "dry bag."** It survives light rain, not submersion. A land title in a torn plastic bag in a flooded cabinet is not protected — it's postponed.
- **Keeping the only copy of anything in one place.** One flood, one fire, or one bag left behind in a rushed evacuation, and it's gone for good. Redundancy is not overkill here.
- **Laminating the actual original of a legal document.** Some agencies require the paper original, unaltered. Laminate a copy instead.
- **Burying the grab-and-go pouch under other things.** If it takes two minutes and a search to find it during an actual evacuation, it has failed its one job.
- **Forgetting the medication list.** In the rush to grab IDs and cash, medical needs get left behind, and pharmacies during a flood response are not reliably stocked or open.

## How to practise safely

Run a dry drill once a season, ideally right before typhoon season starts. Time yourself: from the moment someone says "grab your documents," how long does it take to have the pouch in hand and out the door? Do it once in daylight and once with the lights off, since outages and floods often arrive together.

Check your cloud backup by actually opening it on your phone with no other device nearby — make sure you remember the password and that the files open cleanly. A backup you can't access under stress is not a backup, it's a false sense of security.

If your area has an official flood hazard reference, look it up once and note it down: <a href="https://hazardhunter.georisk.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">HazardHunterPH</a> and <a href="https://noah.up.edu.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Project NOAH</a> both let you check your barangay's exposure, which tells you honestly whether you're in tier-one-heavy territory or can afford to keep more in the box. Pair that knowledge with your barangay's own flood advisories, and this whole system stops being a guess and starts being a plan.
