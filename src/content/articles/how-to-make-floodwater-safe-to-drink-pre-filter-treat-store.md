## What you'll be able to do — and why it matters

After a big typhoon, the tap often keeps running. That's the trap. The water coming out of the pipe can look normal and still not be safe, because floodwater gets into the distribution system through broken lines, low pressure, and submerged valves. <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> will tell you the storm has passed. It won't tell you the water utility is still flushing sediment and sewage out of its own pipes — that can take days, not hours.

This guide walks you through a simple three-step sequence: pre-filter, treat, store. It's the order that matters. Skip a step and you're either wasting tablets on mud or storing treated water in a way that lets it go bad again. None of this is complicated, and none of it needs electricity. That's the point — it has to work when the power and the water pressure are both unreliable.

## What you need

You don't need much, and none of it is expensive. Buy it before typhoon season, not during.

- **A pre-filter.** This can be as simple as a clean cloth, a coffee filter, or several layers of fine mesh over the mouth of a container. Its only job is to take out visible dirt, silt, and floating debris — it doesn't purify anything.
- **Purification tablets.** Aquatabs (chlorine-based) are the common one here, sold in strips or tubs for around ₱200–₱350 depending on tablet count and strength. They kill bacteria, viruses, and most parasites that cause diarrhea and typhoid.
- **A way to boil water as backup.** A gas stove, camping stove, or butane burner. If the brownout runs alongside the flood, this is your fallback when you're out of tablets.
- **Food-grade storage containers.** Covered jerry cans or drums, ₱300–₱600 depending on size, ideally ones sold specifically for water rather than repurposed containers that held chemicals or paint.

All three items show up on Lazada and Shopee. Buy from sellers with a real storefront and reviews, not the lowest-priced listing with no brand name — there are counterfeit purification tablets in circulation that either underdose or don't dissolve properly. If a listing has no expiration date printed on the tablet strip in photos, skip it.

## The method

**Step 1 — Pre-filter first, always.**
If the water is cloudy, muddy, or has visible particles, pour it through cloth or a coffee filter into a clean container before you do anything else. Let it sit for 30 minutes to an hour afterward so heavier sediment settles to the bottom, then pour off the clearer water on top, leaving the sediment behind. This step doesn't make water safe — it makes your next step actually work. Chlorine tablets struggle in turbid water because the chlorine binds to suspended particles instead of killing pathogens.

**Step 2 — Treat what you've pre-filtered.**
For Aquatabs, follow the dosage printed on the packaging — it's usually one tablet per liter for standard strength, but strengths vary by brand, so don't guess. Drop it in, stir, and wait the full contact time stated on the label, typically 30 minutes, before drinking. If you don't have tablets, bring the pre-filtered water to a rolling boil for at least one minute (three minutes if you're above 2,000 meters elevation, which for most Philippine households isn't a concern). Let it cool in a covered container before drinking.

**Step 3 — Store it properly.**
Move treated water into a food-grade, covered container. Keep it elevated off the floor — floodwater can still be sitting or seeping at ground level even after the rain stops, and a container sitting in it can get contaminated again through a loose lid or a hairline crack. Keep it out of direct sunlight, since heat and UV degrade the residual chlorine faster and encourage algae growth in clear containers. Label the container with the date treated. Chlorinated water is generally good for a few days; boiled water should be used within a day if it's not sealed and refrigerated.

## Variations & conditions

**Very turbid or muddy water.** One pass through cloth often isn't enough after a major flood. Filter twice, or let it settle overnight in a covered container before you filter and treat. Don't rush this — it's the step people skip when they're tired and thirsty, and it's the step that determines whether the tablets actually work.

**No stove, no tablets.** If you're caught without either, prioritize getting bottled water or a relief distribution point over drinking untreated floodwater, even briefly boiled water is far safer than nothing, but skipping treatment entirely should be a last resort, not a plan.

**Suspected chemical or fuel contamination.** This is the hard limit of both tablets and boiling, and it's worth saying plainly: neither removes heavy metals, fuel, pesticides, or industrial runoff. If floodwater smells like gasoline, has an oily sheen, or came from an area near a flooded gas station, junk shop, or industrial site, don't try to treat it for drinking at all. Source drinking water elsewhere until the local water district or <a href="https://doh.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Department of Health</a> confirms the supply is safe.

**Extended outages.** In search-and-rescue we never built a plan around one method working — the water plan that held up in the field was the one with a backup that didn't depend on the first method's ingredients running out. Tablets run out. Gas runs out eventually too, but slower, and a stove and a lighter are things most households already have. Keep both options live rather than betting the household on one.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating without pre-filtering.** Dropping a tablet into a jug of muddy water and expecting it to work the way it does on clear water. It doesn't — the chlorine gets used up on the dirt.
- **Not waiting out the full contact time.** Thirty minutes feels long when you're thirsty. Drinking early means the chlorine hasn't finished its job.
- **Storing treated water uncovered or on a wet floor.** This undoes everything you just did. Floodwater residue on the floor can wick into a loosely sealed container.
- **Assuming the tap is safe once the rain stops.** Water utilities in flood-affected areas often issue a boil-water advisory that lasts days after the storm has cleared. Check for local announcements from your barangay or water district before assuming normal service means safe service.
- **Buying tablets during the emergency instead of before it.** Prices go up and stock runs out fast on Lazada and Shopee once a storm is in the news. Buy before the <a href="https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PAGASA</a> bulletins start naming a system headed toward the country, not after.

## How to practise safely

Do a dry run before you actually need it. Fill a container with tap water, deliberately muddy a portion of it with garden soil, and practice the full sequence — filter, settle, treat, store — so the steps are familiar under normal conditions rather than during a brownout with a fussy toddler and low battery on your phone. Time how long the settling and contact steps actually take in your own kitchen. Check your tablet strip's expiration date every typhoon season and replace it if it's aging out, chlorine tablets lose potency over time, especially in humid storage. None of this replaces good judgment about when water simply isn't worth the risk — no filter or tablet replaces sourcing clean water when you have the choice.
